## VIDEO 1 — The Forgetting Curve
**~3 minutos · ~390 palabras**

---

[PANTALLA: abrir el sitio en la sección hero/inicio — dejar que se vea el título "The Cognitive Edge" y el subtítulo]

So — imagine you spend two hours studying for an exam.
You feel good about it. You understood everything.
Then, three days later, you sit down to review
and you can barely remember half of it.

That's not a focus problem. That's not laziness.
That's the Forgetting Curve — and it happens to everyone.

My name is Angel Ramírez, I'm a psychology student at Universidad de Talca,
and this is the first video of my Capstone Project.
The platform is called The Cognitive Edge —
a resource about how memory actually works,
and what you can do about it.

[PANTALLA: hacer scroll suave hasta la sección de la Forgetting Curve con el gráfico interactivo — detente ahí]

In the 1880s, a German psychologist named Hermann Ebbinghaus
ran one of the first controlled studies on human memory.
He memorized lists of meaningless nonsense syllables —
random combinations like "DAX" or "BUP" —
and tested himself at different intervals to see how much he retained.

[PANTALLA: hacer clic en el escenario "No Review" del gráfico — dejar que la curva se dibuje]

What he found was this: memory doesn't fade slowly and evenly.
It drops sharply — exponentially —
with the biggest loss happening within the first twenty-four hours.
Without any review, you lose more than half of what you learned the same day.

[PANTALLA: hacer clic en "Cramming" — mostrar la diferencia]

And the reason for this is actually logical.
The brain constantly clears out information it doesn't access repeatedly,
because storage costs energy.
The problem is it can't tell the difference
between a random fact and something you need for Thursday's exam.

[PANTALLA: hacer clic en "Review after 1 day" y luego "SRS" — mostrar cómo la curva mejora progresivamente]

Now — here's what's interesting.
Even when something feels completely forgotten,
relearning it is still faster than learning it for the first time.
Ebbinghaus called this the savings method.
The neural pathway is still there — weakened, but not erased.

Which means your previous study sessions are never entirely wasted.
You just need to revisit the material before the forgetting gets too deep.

[PANTALLA: scroll hacia arriba, mostrar brevemente la sección de artículos — Articles]

That's exactly what the next two videos are about.
In video two, I'll explain why some students
struggle to encode information even when they're putting in the hours —
and it has nothing to do with intelligence.

## VIDEO 2 — Cognitive Load Theory
**~3 minutos · ~400 palabras**

---

[PANTALLA: abrir el sitio en la sección Articles — Article 02 "Cognitive Load" visible]

Here's a scenario.
You're studying for an important exam.
You've been at it for two hours.
You're tired, there's noise in the background,
your phone keeps lighting up —
and you're reading the same paragraph for the third time
but nothing is sticking.

That's not a willpower problem.
That's what cognitive overload looks like in practice.

[PANTALLA: scroll hasta la sección Labs — Lab "Dual-Task Penalty" visible]

Working memory — the mental space where you process new information —
has a hard limit.
Most people can hold around seven items at once.
When that limit fills up, new information doesn't get stored.
It just gets dropped.

[PANTALLA: hacer clic en "Start Lab" del Dual-Task Penalty — completar una ronda con distracción activada, mostrar el resultado]

This lab demonstrates exactly that.
When you add a secondary task, your performance drops —
not because you stopped trying,
but because the system ran out of space.

[PANTALLA: volver a la sección Articles — Article 02]

A psychologist named John Sweller explained this in 1988
through what's called Cognitive Load Theory.
He identified three types of load that compete for that space.

Intrinsic load — the complexity of the content itself.
Some subjects are just harder. You can't change that.

Extraneous load — everything that surrounds the learning
but doesn't contribute to it.
Noise. Disorganized materials. Constant notifications.
This type consumes resources without producing any learning.

Germane load — the good kind.
The actual mental effort of building and connecting new knowledge.
This is what you want to maximize.

[PANTALLA: hacer scroll lento por el artículo mientras hablas]

The key insight is this:
if extraneous load is eating up your working memory,
there's no room left for germane processing.
Encoding stops.

And here's what makes this harder for a lot of students:
research by Mani and colleagues in 2013 showed
that external stress — financial pressure, personal problems, anxiety —
also consumes working memory.
The cognitive cost is roughly equal to losing a full night of sleep.

So a stressed student is not starting from a neutral baseline.
They're already in deficit before they open a book.

The solution is not to try harder.
It's to reduce the extraneous load:
quiet space, organized materials, phone on airplane mode.
These are not just preferences — they're real cognitive interventions.

[PANTALLA: scroll hacia la sección de Labs]

In the next video, I'll show the study method
that takes all of this into account.

## VIDEO 3 — Spaced Repetition & Active Recall
**~3 minutos · ~410 palabras**

---

[PANTALLA: abrir el sitio en la sección Labs — Lab "Illusion of Competence" visible]

In the first two videos I talked about
why the brain forgets so aggressively,
and why cognitive overload makes learning even harder.

In this one — the solution.

[PANTALLA: hacer clic en el Lab "Illusion of Competence" — iniciar la demostración]

Here's a quick question.
How do you normally study?

Most students re-read their notes, highlight things,
and go over the material until it feels familiar.
And here's the problem:
familiarity is not the same as recall.

[PANTALLA: completar el lab — mostrar cómo resaltar no mejora el recall en las preguntas]

When you re-read something, your brain recognizes the words
and produces a sense of "yeah, I know this."
That feeling is real — but it's misleading.
Because on an exam, the textbook is not in front of you.
You need actual recall, not recognition.

Psychologists call this the illusion of competence.
It's one of the most common traps in academic studying.

[PANTALLA: ir al Lab "Chunking Protocol"]

The fix is active recall —
and it's exactly what it sounds like.
Instead of reading the material again,
you close your notes and try to retrieve it from memory.
Make a flashcard. Do a practice test before you feel ready.
Write down everything you remember without looking.

[PANTALLA: completar una ronda del Chunking Protocol — mostrar el resultado]

The difficulty you feel when doing this —
that struggle, almost failing to remember —
that is the learning happening.
Research by Roediger and Karpicke in 2006
showed that students using active recall retained fifty percent more
after one week than students who re-read for the same amount of time.

Not because they studied more. Because they studied differently.

[PANTALLA: volver a la sección Articles — Article 03 "Spaced Repetition" visible]

Now combine this with spaced repetition:
instead of reviewing everything the night before the exam,
you do short review sessions over days or weeks —
each one timed to catch information just before it fades.

Tools like Anki do this automatically.
You rate each flashcard — Easy, Good, Hard, Again —
and the app decides when to show it to you next.
Ten to fifteen minutes a day, compounding over time.

[PANTALLA: scroll hasta el footer/final del sitio]

The forgetting curve is not something you push through by trying harder.
But it is something you can work around —
if you understand how memory functions
and use strategies that are actually designed for it.

This platform was built to make those strategies accessible.
Thanks for watching.
